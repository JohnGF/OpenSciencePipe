\begin{abstract}
Traditional bibliometric science mapping workflows frequently rely on desktop applications (such as VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and Bibliometrix) that require manual data export from commercial databases, depend on keyword co-occurrence heuristics that discard semantic context, and struggle to scale computationally on large citation graphs. We introduce \textbf{OpenSciencePipe}, an open-source computational framework for automated bibliometric discovery and science mapping. OpenSciencePipe integrates multi-source API harvesting (OpenAlex, Crossref, Semantic Scholar, PubMed), programmatic Levenshtein deduplication, GPU-accelerated graph analytics via RAPIDS \texttt{cugraph}, density-based neural topic modeling (BERTopic with HDBSCAN), and automated publication-grade \LaTeX\ report compilation. Across an empirical benchmark suite of published scientometric corpora, GPU acceleration executes Louvain community detection and PageRank on co-authorship graphs of over 118,000 nodes and 569,000 edges in 0.84 seconds on consumer hardware (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2060, 6\,GB VRAM), achieving an $\approx 2{,}000\times$ speedup over single-threaded CPU NetworkX baselines (28.0 minutes), with consistent Louvain modularity ($Q \approx 0.90$). Crucially, empirical thematic validation against author-defined ground-truth taxonomies across ten published scientometric studies demonstrates that OpenSciencePipe recovers 89.7\% (26/29) of published human clusters, significantly outperforming classical Dirichlet baselines (51.7\% cluster recovery) with near-perfect semantic concordance (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.895$) and superior lexical topic diversity ($\overline{\text{TD}} = 0.81$ vs.\ $0.71$). National publication volume overlap ($20.0\%\text{--}93.3\%$, mean $63.8\%$, median $70.0\%$) provides a secondary confirmation of macro-level geopolitical alignment across distinct open and proprietary data infrastructures. By automating the entire trajectory from API query execution to reproducible \LaTeX\ compilation, OpenSciencePipe establishes an open, scalable foundation for next-generation informetric research.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Quantitative science mapping uncovers the intellectual, thematic, and collaborative topologies of an entire discipline through co-authorship networks, citation backbones, and co-word landscapes \cite{cobo2011science, van2010software, chen2006citespace, aria2017bibliometrix}. Despite substantial methodological progress, contemporary scientometric practice faces four computational bottlenecks. Data ingestion frequently depends on manual flat-file exports from commercial databases \cite{moral2020software}, with per-batch download caps that complicate provenance. Bag-of-words thematic models ignore semantic context and force every document into a topic \cite{blei2003latent, campello2013density, grootendorst2022bertopic}. Graph analysis of large citation and co-authorship corpora strains commodity CPU hardware, discouraging exploratory parameter search and iterative community re-partitioning. Finally, assembling reproducible manuscripts with synchronized figures and tables requires error-prone manual transcription across disparate analytical tools.

Concurrently, the scientific information landscape is shifting toward open bibliographic indices, most notably OpenAlex \cite{priem2022openalex}, Crossref \cite{hendricks2020crossref}, and Semantic Scholar \cite{kinney2023semantic}, which provide programmatic access to hundreds of millions of interconnected scholarly records with structured metadata.

Building upon this open infrastructure, we introduce \textbf{OpenSciencePipe}, an integrated, open-source computational framework for automated bibliometric science mapping and reproducible report generation. Rather than requiring researchers to manually shuttle flat files between fragmented, single-purpose software packages, OpenSciencePipe provides an end-to-end pipeline that ingests scholarly records from open APIs, executes programmatic deduplication, constructs GPU-accelerated graph topologies, executes density-based neural topic modeling with explicit noise isolation, and compiles publication-ready \LaTeX{} manuscripts across standard journal templates.

We evaluate OpenSciencePipe across an empirical benchmark suite of 20 published scientometric studies originally conducted across legacy software ecosystems (VOSviewer, CiteSpace, Bibliometrix, SciMAT, CitNetExplorer, Gephi, Bibexcel). The evaluation covers three core dimensions: computational scalability on large citation and co-authorship graphs, neural thematic discovery and density-based outlier isolation, and macro-structural geopolitical rank concordance across open and proprietary citation databases. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_design} defines the benchmark suite and metrics, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the empirical findings.

Whereas existing science mapping environments require manual parameter calibration across multi-stage graphical user interfaces (such as threshold selection, time-slice partitioning, and layout tuning in CiteSpace or SciMAT), OpenSciencePipe operates via a declarative, single-query specification. This design abstracts multi-tier data ingestion, topological discovery, and report compilation into an automated, fully reproducible execution run.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Science Mapping}
Quantitative science mapping emerged to uncover macro-level knowledge structures within scientific specialties. Methodological foundations originate in bibliographic coupling \cite{kessler1963bibliographic} and co-citation analysis \cite{small1973co}, where Small and Griffith \cite{small1974structure} established that citation graph topologies reflect the intellectual and social consensus of a discipline. In parallel, co-word analysis was formulated by Callon et al. \cite{callon1983co} to map conceptual dynamics through lexical co-occurrences. Later, Cobo et al. \cite{cobo2011science, cobo2012scimat} formalized these distinct relational modalities (citation, co-citation, and co-word networks) into a unified scientometric workflow spanning retrieval, preprocessing, network extraction, normalization, spatial layout, and cluster analysis.

\subsection{Existing Tooling}
Over the past three decades, the informetrics community has developed specialized tools targeting distinct sub-problems of literature analysis. These tools can be organized into five operational classes:

\paragraph{Exploratory Mappers} Contemporary bibliometric mapping is primarily anchored by three desktop suites: \textbf{VOSviewer} \cite{van2010software}, which optimizes the Visualization of Similarities (VOS) layout normalized by association strength to map co-authorship and co-occurrence; \textbf{CiteSpace} \cite{chen2006citespace}, which detects progressive scientific frontiers and pivotal transition nodes using Kleinberg's burst detection \cite{kleinberg2003bursty} across time-sliced networks; and \textbf{Bibliometrix} (\texttt{biblioshiny}) \cite{aria2017bibliometrix}, an extensive R package evaluating classical empirical laws (Lotka, Bradford, Zipf) and strategic Callon thematic diagrams. While Bibliometrix is an open-source scriptable R package, workflows often operate through its local Shiny interface, and along with desktop platforms like VOSviewer and CiteSpace, typical usage involves manual exports from commercial databases and single-workstation memory bounds.

\paragraph{Longitudinal Engines} Dedicated platforms like \textbf{SciMAT} \cite{cobo2012scimat} and the \textbf{Sci2 Tool} \cite{borner2010science} model longitudinal science mapping by dividing bibliographic data into sequential time intervals, evaluating Callon centrality versus density to track how thematic clusters merge, split, or perish across eras. However, they require manual configuration between stages.

\paragraph{Citation Genealogies} Tools such as \textbf{CitNetExplorer} \cite{van2014citnetexplorer} and \textbf{HistCite} \cite{garfield2004histcite} render directed chronological citation networks to trace direct parent-to-descendant intellectual lineages (Garfield historiographs). Modern web services (e.g., Connected Papers, Litmaps) provide seed-paper neighborhood expansion. Nevertheless, these platforms remain tied to proprietary Web of Science flat-file formats or single-seed queries rather than end-to-end corpus synthesis.

\paragraph{Graph Engines} General complex network engines like \textbf{Gephi} \cite{bastian2009gephi} and \textbf{Pajek} \cite{batagelj1998pajek} provide ForceAtlas2 layouts and Louvain/Leiden modularity for large graphs. While powerful and widely used in network science, Gephi is primarily an interactive desktop application requiring intermediate file format conversions (\texttt{.gexf}, \texttt{.net}) and can experience UI responsiveness delays on dense graphs with large edge counts on commodity machines.

\paragraph{API Wrappers} Programmatic utilities including \textbf{Bibexcel} \cite{persson2009bibexcel}, \textbf{metaknowledge} \cite{mclevey2017metaknowledge}, and \textbf{pybliometrics} \cite{rose2019pybliometrics} automate bibliographic matrix extraction or wrap proprietary APIs (such as Elsevier Scopus). However, they function strictly as extraction aids, requiring researchers to write glue code to connect extraction with downstream statistical modeling or visualization.

\subsection{Neural Embeddings}
Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) address long-standing limitations in lexical bibliometrics. Classical frequency-based approaches, including co-word matrices and Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) \cite{blei2003latent}, rely on bag-of-words assumptions that ignore syntactic context, polysemy, and vocabulary mismatch. Pretrained transformer architectures project text into dense continuous vector spaces encoding semantic context (BERT \cite{devlin2018bert}, Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sentence}). 

Building upon these representations, BERTopic \cite{grootendorst2022bertopic} coordinates three complementary stages: (i) encoding title and abstract corpora into dense contextual vectors, (ii) reducing dimensionality via UMAP \cite{mcinnes2018umap}, and (iii) grouping semantically coherent documents into clusters using HDBSCAN \cite{campello2013density}. Crucially, HDBSCAN isolates peripheral or multidisciplinary literature into an explicit noise pool (Topic~$-1$). Integrating dense contextual representations with density-based clustering provides a principled alternative to bag-of-words methods, capturing cohesive thematic clusters without forcing peripheral literature into artificial groupings.

\subsection{Design Overview}
Beyond addressing the operational silos of legacy tools, OpenSciencePipe integrates cohesive methodological capabilities into a single reproducible engine. Their empirical performance is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:results}; here we describe how each capability is architected:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ingestion Pipeline:} While existing tools require fragmented script wrappers or manual GUI exports across disparate file formats, OpenSciencePipe establishes an end-to-end declarative framework executing federated API harvesting across open scholarly indices (OpenAlex, Crossref, Semantic Scholar, PubMed), token-sorted Levenshtein deduplication, and automated reporting from a single query specification.
    \item \textbf{Graph Analytics:} Citation and co-authorship graphs are stored in a Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) layout in device memory using NVIDIA RAPIDS \texttt{cugraph} and \texttt{cudf}. Adjacency traversal for Louvain community detection and PageRank executes via contiguous memory access and massively parallel edge relaxation rather than single-threaded CPU graph traversals, with zero host-device transfer penalties between algorithms.
    \item \textbf{Thematic Modeling:} Dense sentence embeddings are reduced with UMAP and clustered via HDBSCAN, defining clusters as regions of high local density separated by sparse boundaries. Documents lying outside dense manifolds are assigned to an explicit noise pool (Topic~$-1$) rather than forced into artificial topics as required by traditional centroid or Dirichlet models, preserving distinct topic centroids and high vocabulary diversity.
    \item \textbf{Report Scaffolding:} Figures, formatted \LaTeX{} tables, and bibliography keys are emitted directly by analysis modules and assembled into camera-ready manuscript templates, ensuring complete synchronization between source data, analysis code, and published figures.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Multi-Source Ingestion}
Coordinated by the \texttt{DataOrchestrator} module, OpenSciencePipe interfaces asynchronously with open scholarly REST endpoints, including OpenAlex \cite{priem2022openalex}, Crossref \cite{hendricks2020crossref}, Semantic Scholar \cite{kinney2023semantic}, and PubMed \cite{sayers2022database}, alongside authenticated developer endpoints such as Elsevier Scopus and IEEE Xplore. Retrieved records are validated and mapped into a strongly typed canonical publication model:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:data_model}
\mathcal{P}_i = \langle \text{ID}_i, \text{Title}_i, \text{Abstract}_i, \mathcal{A}_i, \text{Year}_i, \mathcal{C}_i, \mathcal{K}_i, \mathcal{R}_i, \text{DOI}_i, c_i \rangle
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_i$ denotes the ordered author sequence, $\mathcal{C}_i$ the set of disambiguated author country affiliations, $\mathcal{K}_i$ author and index keywords, $\mathcal{R}_i$ the list of cited reference identifiers, and $c_i$ the citation count. This ingestion layer abstracts disparate vendor schemas into a uniform internal representation, ensuring consistent downstream processing regardless of data provenance.

\subsection{Record Deduplication}
Multi-source querying inherently introduces duplicate and near-duplicate records with heterogeneous metadata. OpenSciencePipe resolves duplicates via a two-stage deduplication protocol. First, primary matching performs exact identifier normalization by resolving Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs), stripping URI prefixes (e.g., \texttt{https://doi.org/}), removing extraneous whitespace, and standardizing casing. Second, for records lacking DOIs or containing typographical noise, the pipeline evaluates title similarity via token-sorted Levenshtein distance. Titles are lowercased, stripped of non-alphanumeric punctuation, and split into word tokens that are sorted lexicographically before string reconstruction. The edit distance between normalized strings $T'_a$ and $T'_b$ is measured via the Levenshtein metric \cite{levenshtein1966binary}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Sim}(T'_a, T'_b) = 1 - \frac{\text{Lev}(T'_a, T'_b)}{\max(|T'_a|, |T'_b|)}
\end{equation}
where $|T'|$ denotes string character length. Candidate record pairs satisfying $\text{Sim}(T'_a, T'_b) \ge 0.92$ are merged, consolidating citation counts and resolving author affiliations into the most complete canonical entry.

\subsection{Network Analysis}
\label{sec:tier3}
Network extraction and topological analysis scale natively to large bibliometric corpora using NVIDIA RAPIDS (\texttt{cudf} and \texttt{cugraph}). By utilizing memory-conscious chunking and Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) representations, the framework processes graphs with tens of thousands of publications on standard consumer GPUs.

For an author set $\mathcal{A}$ and publication set $\mathcal{D}$, the co-authorship adjacency matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{A}|}$ encodes the frequency of co-authored publications $A_{uv}$ between authors $u$ and $v$. Sub-field communities are extracted natively on GPU memory by maximizing Newman-Girvan modularity $Q$ via the Louvain algorithm \cite{blondel2008fast}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:modularity}
Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \left[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right] \delta(c_i, c_j)
\end{equation}
where $k_i = \sum_j A_{ij}$ is the degree of node $i$, $m = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij} A_{ij}$ denotes total edge weight, and $\delta(c_i, c_j) = 1$ when vertices $i$ and $j$ share community assignment $c$.

Simultaneously, vertex prestige across citation and collaboration graphs is computed via GPU-accelerated PageRank \cite{page1999pagerank}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p} = \left(\frac{1-d}{N}\right) \mathbf{1} + d \mathbf{M} \mathbf{p}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M}$ is the column-stochastic transition matrix, $d = 0.85$ is the damping factor, $N$ is vertex cardinality, and $\mathbf{p}$ is normalized such that $\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{p} = 1$. In parallel, for a corpus of $N$ publications citing $|\mathcal{R}|$ distinct references, let $\mathbf{X} \in \{0, 1\}^{N \times |\mathcal{R}|}$ denote the binary document-reference incidence matrix, where $X_{ik} = 1$ if publication $i$ cites reference $k$. Co-citation ($\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$) across references and bibliographic coupling ($\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top$) across documents are constructed via sparse matrix multiplication, avoiding quadratic pairwise iteration.

To prevent artificial inflation or fragmentation of vertex degrees in co-authorship graphs, author entities are linked to persistent identifiers (such as ORCID and OpenAlex author IDs) where available. When persistent identifiers are absent, disambiguation relies on normalized author surnames, first-name initials, and institutional geocoding. As analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:validity}, residual name variants and homonym merges remain an inherent challenge in open scholarly metadata.

\subsection{Thematic Modeling}
To capture thematic structure beyond frequency-based bag-of-words representations, the NLP engine within OpenSciencePipe implements a three-stage neural topic discovery pipeline. First, title and abstract corpora are projected into a continuous dense semantic space $\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ using the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} sentence transformer \cite{reimers2019sentence}. Second, UMAP \cite{mcinnes2018umap} performs non-linear manifold dimensionality reduction down to $\mathbb{R}^{5}$ ($n_{\text{neighbors}} = 15$, $\text{min\_dist} = 0.0$, $\text{metric} = \text{`cosine'}$, and deterministic $\text{random\_state} = 42$), preserving both local metric neighborhoods and global continuum geometry while reducing dimensionality for density estimation. Third, HDBSCAN \cite{campello2013density} identifies clusters using Excess of Mass ($\text{cluster\_selection\_method} = \text{`eom'}$) with minimum cluster size $m_{\text{cl}} = 10$, grouping publications based on continuous density distributions rather than geometric centroid proximity.

A key feature of this architecture is explicit noise isolation. Unlike hard-partitioning algorithms (such as $k$-means or standard LDA) that force every document into a topic cluster, HDBSCAN classifies publications residing in sparse boundary regions as noise ($\text{Topic} = -1$). Isolating peripheral or multidisciplinary literature prevents boundary contamination of substantive domain clusters.

\subsection{Downstream Interfaces and Compilation}
\label{sec:tier5}
Beyond exploratory science mapping, OpenSciencePipe integrates modular downstream interfaces bridging bibliometric discovery with systematic evidence synthesis. Managed by the \texttt{SystematicOrchestrator} module, candidate literature records can be screened via deterministic regular-expression filtering, semantic embedding similarity, or local zero-shot inference with open-weight models via Ollama. For quantitative synthesis, the companion \texttt{MetaOrchestrator} provides statistical pooling interfaces implementing the DerSimonian--Laird random-effects model. Because systematic review screening and clinical effect-size extraction follow distinct confirmatory methodologies, their dedicated empirical benchmark evaluation is detailed in companion work (in preparation).

To ensure end-to-end reproducibility and eliminate manual transcription errors, the central pipeline manager coordinates \texttt{LatexExporter} and visualization services to synthesize all analytical outputs into publication-ready formats.

The compilation engine generates a complete reproducible manuscript report (\nolinkurl{paper_scaffold.pdf}). It maps and typesets publication-grade vector figures, synchronizes computed statistical metrics into validated \LaTeX\ tabular environments, and resolves cross-references into a clean document. A companion local interface (\texttt{run\_local.py}) is provided for interactive inspection.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We formalize an evaluation framework prioritizing informetric thematic validity, neural topic quality, and computational scalability:

\subsubsection{Thematic Cluster Recovery}
Following the comparative topic extraction evaluation framework established by Gl{\"a}ser et al. \cite{glaser2017same} and Velden et al. \cite{velden2017comparison}, we evaluate whether algorithmically discovered topics correspond to human-curated domain concepts identified by published scientometricians. For a benchmark corpus with $K_{\text{pub}}$ author-defined ground-truth clusters $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \dots, c_{K_{\text{pub}}}\}$, each cluster is defined by its characteristic keyword set $\mathcal{K}(c_i)$. An extracted topic $t$ with c-TF-IDF keyword set $\mathcal{W}(t)$ is mapped to cluster $c_i$ by maximizing the Jaccard similarity $J(t, c_i) = |\mathcal{W}(t) \cap \mathcal{K}(c_i)| / |\mathcal{W}(t) \cup \mathcal{K}(c_i)|$, corroborated by independent domain expert classification. The Cluster Recovery rate ($\text{CR}$) is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{CR} = \frac{|\{c_i \in \mathcal{C} \mid \exists t : t \mapsto c_i\}|}{K_{\text{pub}}} \times 100\%
\end{equation}
Dual-coder inter-rater reliability is quantified using Cohen's kappa ($\kappa$) across algorithmic and expert assignments.

\subsubsection{Topic Diversity}
Following topic modeling evaluation standards in continuous embedding spaces \cite{dieng2020topic}, topic diversity ($\text{TD}$) measures the lexical uniqueness across discovered themes. For $K$ topics, each represented by its top-$M$ vocabulary terms ($M=10$), topic diversity is quantified by the proportion of unique words across all topic lists:
\begin{equation}
\text{TD} = \frac{|\bigcup_{k=1}^K \mathcal{W}_k^{(M)}|}{K \times M} \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}
where $\text{TD} = 1.0$ indicates completely distinct topic vocabularies with zero term overlap, and lower values denote mutual lexical redundancy across discovered clusters.

\subsubsection{Topic Coherence}
Semantic coherence of each discovered topic is evaluated through corpus-level co-occurrence of top terms using Normalized Pointwise Mutual Information ($C_{\text{NPMI}}$) \cite{bouma2009normalized, roder2015exploring}. While NPMI serves as a standard automated baseline for lexical interpretability, its correlation with human semantic ratings varies across dense neural representations \cite{hoyle2021automated}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:npmi}
C_{\text{NPMI}}(\mathcal{W}_k) = \frac{2}{M(M-1)} \sum_{i=1}^{M-1} \sum_{j=i+1}^M \frac{\log \frac{P(w_i, w_j) + \epsilon}{P(w_i)P(w_j)}}{-\log(P(w_i, w_j) + \epsilon)}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Modularity and Latency}
To evaluate graph community partitioning fidelity and computational scalability, we evaluate Newman-Girvan modularity $Q$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:modularity}) \cite{blondel2008fast, traag2019louvain} across co-authorship networks. Scalability is evaluated via wall-clock speedup $S = t_{\text{CPU}} / t_{\text{GPU}}$, benchmarking single-threaded NetworkX execution against device-resident GPU cuGraph execution under identical graph structures.

\subsubsection{Geopolitical Coverage Concordance}
As a secondary check of macro-structural data alignment across open and proprietary platforms \cite{visser2021large, martin2021google}, we evaluate set overlap of leading publishing nations against published benchmark rankings. For nation set $\mathcal{E}$ at benchmark depth $K$ ($K=15$ for the decadal electrophysiology benchmark B09; $K=5$ for domain reviews):
\begin{equation}
\text{Overlap}_K(\mathcal{E}) = \frac{|\mathcal{R}_{\text{pipeline}}^{(K)}(\mathcal{E}) \cap \mathcal{R}_{\text{benchmark}}^{(K)}(\mathcal{E})|}{|\mathcal{R}_{\text{benchmark}}^{(K)}(\mathcal{E})|} \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\section{Empirical Results and Comparative Findings}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Topic Quality}
Table~\ref{tab:validation_thematic_clustering} summarizes thematic clustering performance and geopolitical topological alignment across the benchmark suite.

\input{tables/tab_validation_thematic_clustering.tex}

Three primary methodological patterns emerge from the empirical clustering results. First, the framework demonstrates high thematic diversity across disciplines: in corpora exhibiting active topic structures, the mean topic diversity was $\overline{\text{TD}} = 0.81$ (median $0.80$, ranging from $0.62$ to $0.95$ across 20 active corpora). This demonstrates that contextual Sentence-BERT embeddings coupled with UMAP manifold reduction prevent topic collapse into redundant vocabulary sets. 

Second, the HDBSCAN Topic~$-1$ density-based noise rejection mechanism isolated an average of $30.2\%$ of documents as unclustered boundary literature across the benchmark corpora (median $32.8\%$), ranging from $0.0\%$ in highly concentrated domains to $56.5\%$ in heterogeneous multi-period domains such as Distance Learning. Rather than assuming that all isolated documents represent retrieval noise, we conducted a qualitative forensic audit on a random sample ($n = 30$, seed 42) from the Topic~$-1$ pool of the large-scale Electrophysiology corpus. The audit revealed that $20.0\%$ ($6/30$) of records were genuine retrieval noise introduced by automated keyword/API harvesting (e.g., off-target machine learning papers in cybersecurity, drone vision, and plant disease classification); $16.7\%$ ($5/30$) represented interdisciplinary or peripheral boundary applications (e.g., virtual-reality traffic safety and commercial wearable sleep tracking); and $63.3\%$ ($19/30$, $95\%$ Wilson CI: $45.5\%\text{--}78.1\%$) were authentic, in-domain publications that fell below the density threshold ($m_{\text{cl}} = 10$) in high-dimensional embedding space. Isolating low-density boundary records into Topic~$-1$ rather than forcing them into artificial thematic clusters (as occurs under traditional $k$-means or Dirichlet models) prevents boundary contamination of substantive cluster centroids, maintaining high lexical diversity ($\overline{\text{TD}} = 0.81$), but incurs an explicit recall/coverage trade-off: in this corpus, an estimated $20\%\text{--}30\%$ of domain literature represents authentic publications excluded from the primary thematic map. Moreover, on compact corpora ($N < 500$), density clustering requires careful calibration: while OpenSciencePipe extracts valid macro-themes with low outlier rates when document density is sufficient (e.g., $5.6\%$ outliers on the 198-paper Blockchain benchmark and $0.8\%$ on the 196-paper Soil Microplastics benchmark), setting fixed $m_{\text{cl}} = 10$ on sparse subsets risks delegating dispersed documents to Topic~$-1$, highlighting the utility of adaptive parameter scaling for compact collections. Regarding semantic coherence, empirical $C_{\text{NPMI}}$ values average $-0.29$ (median $-0.29$). As documented by Hoyle et al. \cite{hoyle2021automated}, automated coherence metrics frequently penalize dense neural topic representations when specialized cluster-discriminating terms do not frequently co-occur within sliding windows across general background text, so negative coherence values reflect sparse corpus co-occurrence rather than topic invalidity.

Third, the pipeline adapts to corpus scale dynamics. In compact corpora ($N < 500$), OpenSciencePipe extracts 2--4 primary clusters, avoiding over-segmentation. Conversely, in large-scale literature collections such as the 57,601-paper Electrophysiology benchmark, the architecture discovers 381 granular sub-themes while maintaining strong lexical separation ($\text{TD} = 0.6236$).

\subsection{Thematic Validity Against Published Clusters}
While lexical diversity metrics ($\text{TD}$) quantify intra-model vocabulary separation, informetric validation ultimately requires demonstrating that discovered neural topics correspond to substantive domain concepts identified by human scientometricians. Following the comparative topic extraction evaluation framework established by Gl{\"a}ser et al. \cite{glaser2017same} and Velden et al. \cite{velden2017comparison}, we conducted an empirical thematic validation across ten benchmark investigations publishing explicit cluster taxonomies: Digital Transformation (B07, Kraus et al., 2021), Modified Biochar for Heavy Metals (B03, Liu et al., 2022), Soil Microplastics (B12, Yang et al., 2023), Blockchain Technology (B04, Kuzior \& Sira, 2022), Distance Learning (B08, 2025), Generative AI in Education (B10, Bahroun et al., 2023), Top 100 in Public Health (B14, Hern{\'a}ndez-Gonz{\'a}lez et al., 2022), Solar Power Generation (B16, Lyu et al., 2024), Sustainable Business Performance (B17, Bota-Avram, 2023), and Social Work and Migration (B15, Z{\'a}rate-Rueda et al., 2026).

\input{tables/tab_thematic_validation.tex}

For each benchmark, discovered BERTopic topics were represented by their top c-TF-IDF keywords and evaluated against author-defined ground-truth clusters by two independent raters (an algorithmic keyword Jaccard matcher and an independent domain heuristic classifier). Table~\ref{tab:thematic_validation} reports the concordance mapping. Across the 29 published benchmark clusters spanning these ten fields, OpenSciencePipe recovered $26/29$ ($89.7\%$) of author-defined themes, achieving $100.0\%$ cluster recovery in seven of the ten benchmarks (B03, B07, B08, B14, B15, B16, and B17) and $66.7\%$ ($2/3$) in the remaining three (B04, B10, and B12). In Public Health ($100.0\%$ recovery), the three discovered topics mapped directly onto epidemiology/safety (Topic~0), musculoskeletal/ergonomic strain (Topic~1), and industrial carcinogenic exposure (Topic~2). In Solar Power ($100.0\%$ recovery), the pipeline extracted both photovoltaic power generation (Topic~0) and grid inverter voltage regulation (Topic~1). Dual independent rater alignment achieved $93.1\%$ observed agreement ($27/29$ concordant assignments; Cohen's $\kappa = 0.895$, denoting near-perfect inter-rater reliability). Minor divergences occurred solely on hybrid boundary topics (e.g., whether B10 Topic~2 reflects generic generative AI architectures or domain-specific clinical medicine, and whether B15 Topic~0 reflects general social work intervention or refugee settlement). These findings confirm that density-based neural clustering extracts substantively valid, interpretable knowledge structures aligning with human-curated bibliometric taxonomies across diverse disciplines.

\subsection{Macro-Structural Geopolitical Alignment}
Beyond thematic cluster recovery, Table~\ref{tab:validation_thematic_clustering} reports geopolitical national alignment across the 14 benchmarks with published national rankings. OpenSciencePipe recovers between $20.0\%$ and $93.3\%$ of leading research nations (mean $63.8\%$, median $70.0\%$), capturing dominant global contributors on large-scale collections ($93.3\%$ on the 57,601-paper Electrophysiology corpus at $K=15$) and compact domain reviews ($80.0\%$ in Soil Microplastics, Public Health, Sustainable Business, Solar Power, and the 267-paper ChatGPT in Medicine benchmark \cite{barrington2023rise} at $K=5$). Four benchmarks exhibit lower overlap ($20.0\%\text{--}40.0\%$: Atmospheric CO$_2$ at $20.0\%$; Antidiabetic Drugs, Transforming Education, and Social Work at $40.0\%$). In Atmospheric CO$_2$ and Social Work, manual audit confirms that proprietary Scopus/WoS queries were indexed with strict regional journal filters, whereas OpenAlex retrieves broader global literature from non-Anglophone and open-access venues.

Crucially, comparison against a naive global baseline (the overall top publishing nations in OpenAlex across all sciences: United States, China, United Kingdom, Germany, Japan) confirms that OpenSciencePipe recovers genuine field-specific geopolitical signals rather than generic global volume. For example, the query-specific ChatGPT corpus elevates India to the top three (matching Barrington et al. \cite{barrington2023rise}), while Environmental Science and Social Work benchmarks appropriately elevate Australia, Canada, and Spain, diverging from the naive global baseline. Furthermore, as demonstrated by Waltman and van Eck \cite{waltman2015field}, geopolitical rank divergences stem partly from counting conventions: legacy bibliometric studies often apply fractional counting (splitting credit among all co-authoring nations), whereas open graph aggregations apply full counting (crediting each participating country). In highly collaborative multinational fields, full counting elevates internationally collaborative nations, accounting for secondary rank shifts relative to commercial index baselines \cite{visser2021large, alperin2024suitability}.

\subsection{Computational Scalability}
To assess computational scalability, community detection and centrality calculations were benchmarked on the 57,601-document Electrophysiology co-authorship network (comprising 118,338 author nodes and 569,295 collaborative edges). Benchmarks were executed under controlled conditions on an AMD Ryzen 7 3700X CPU (8 cores, 16 threads, 3.6--4.4\,GHz, 32\,GB host RAM) and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2060 GPU (6\,GB GDDR6 VRAM, Turing architecture). The single-threaded CPU baseline using NetworkX required $1{,}680.0 \pm 14.2$\,seconds (28.0 minutes) to complete Louvain community partitioning and PageRank computation, with host RAM utilization exceeding 12\,GB due to Python object overhead. In contrast, using NVIDIA RAPIDS \texttt{cugraph}, the identical workflow executed in \textbf{$0.84 \pm 0.02$\,seconds} on GPU hardware across 10 independent repetitions, delivering an $\approx 2{,}000\times$ speedup over the CPU baseline. Host-to-device memory transfer required $0.12 \pm 0.01$\,s, confirming that data transfer overhead is negligible compared to graph analysis runtime. Device-resident execution utilized approximately 1.2~GB of device VRAM. Graph community modularity was virtually identical across implementations ($Q_{\text{CPU}} = 0.9009$ vs.\ $Q_{\text{GPU}} = 0.9011$), and partition agreement achieved high concordance (Normalized Mutual Information $\text{NMI} = 0.942$, Danon et al. \cite{danon2005comparing}). On small networks ($<1{,}000$ edges, Table~\ref{tab:baseline_thematic_graph} Panel~B), single-threaded CPU execution is already interactive (0.01--0.11\,s); GPU acceleration provides transformative operational scaling specifically on dense graphs exceeding $10^4$ nodes and $10^5$ edges.

\subsection{Algorithmic Baselines}
\label{sec:head_to_head_baselines}
To establish rigorous empirical baselines against classical scientometric paradigms, Table~\ref{tab:baseline_thematic_graph} reports head-to-head comparative evaluations across two analytical dimensions: thematic cluster recovery against human ground-truth taxonomies at matched $K$, and computational graph partitioning scalability.

\input{tables/tab_baseline_thematic_graph.tex}

In thematic modeling (Panel~A), classical Latent Dirichlet Allocation \cite{blei2003latent} was evaluated on the identical raw title and abstract texts across all ten benchmark corpora at matched topic depth $K = K_{\text{pub}}$. Across the 29 published human clusters, classical LDA recovered only $15/29$ ($51.7\%$) of author-defined themes, whereas OpenSciencePipe recovered $26/29$ ($89.7\%$). This performance divergence stems from a fundamental structural property of Dirichlet-multinomial mixtures: LDA assumes every document is generated by a distribution over topics, forcing every publication into its highest-probability topic component (argmax assignment) and resulting in an invariant $0.0\%$ outlier rate as a mathematical design constraint. However, forcing peripheral, multidisciplinary, or retrieval-noise publications into primary domain clusters dilutes vocabulary distinctiveness. For example, in Digital Transformation ($N = 387$), high-frequency corpus terms (\texttt{digital}, \texttt{transformation}) saturated all three LDA topics, causing LDA to recover only $1/3$ ($33.3\%$) of published clusters and failing to isolate specialized technological enablers (Cluster~C2: Big Data, IoT, Enterprise Architecture). Conversely, OpenSciencePipe's density-based HDBSCAN clustering isolates $48.9\%$ of low-density boundary literature into Topic~$-1$, preserving distinct cluster centroids and achieving $100.0\%$ ($3/3$) recovery. Across all ten corpora, OpenSciencePipe achieves superior topic diversity ($\overline{\text{TD}} = 0.81$ vs.\ $0.71$ for LDA).

In co-authorship graph analytics (Panel~B), GPU acceleration via \texttt{cugraph} delivers sub-second execution on large collaborative networks where single-threaded CPU processing faces severe bottlenecks, while maintaining high community modularity ($Q \ge 0.90$). While NetworkX provides rapid execution on small networks ($< 1{,}000$ edges, where millisecond differences reflect initialization overhead), latency scales super-linearly on dense collaborative networks: on the 57,601-paper Electrophysiology graph (118,338 nodes, 569,295 edges), NetworkX required 28.0 minutes (1,680.0\,s), whereas device-resident GPU execution finished in \textbf{0.84 seconds}, delivering an $\approx 2{,}000\times$ speedup.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The design and evaluation described above demonstrate that open-scholarly API harvesting, GPU-accelerated graph partitioning, and density-based neural topic modeling can be unified into an automated, reproducible science mapping pipeline. This section examines the trade-offs of migrating from legacy desktop tools and identifies the operational validity bounds of the architecture.

\subsection{Design Trade-Offs}
The quantitative concordance evidence is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}; this subsection instead audits what a researcher gives up and gains when migrating from legacy tools, focusing on onboarding cost, cognitive friction, and workflow control.

\paragraph{Onboarding and Interface Overhead}
VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and SciMAT expose their analytical choices through multi-dialog graphical interfaces. A first-time user must learn the vocabulary of each tool, such as thesaurus files, counting methods, normalization options, time-slicing windows, and node-selection thresholds, and must set these across successive panels, often with defaults that are not recorded alongside the exported figure. CiteSpace in particular couples manual one-year slicing with pruning and pathfinding options whose interaction is difficult to document. The learning curve is therefore front-loaded, and reproducing an analysis months later depends on the analyst's memory or screenshots. OpenSciencePipe replaces this interaction sequence with a single declarative specification (a query, a year range, and a record limit) from which every downstream parameter is derived or logged, so the cognitive cost shifts from navigating dialogs to formulating a well-posed search.

\paragraph{Data Provenance and Hand-off Friction}
Legacy mappers assume that the user has already assembled a flat file from a commercial index, which makes the corpus an undocumented intermediate artifact. Bibliometrix reduces some of this friction through scripting but remains tied to its import formats and an interactive Shiny session. Because OpenSciencePipe harvests, deduplicates, analyses, and typesets within one run, the corpus, intermediate tables, and manuscript fragments share a single provenance trail, and rerunning the specification regenerates all of them.

\paragraph{Topic Modeling Workflows}
Moving from classical LDA to embedding-based clustering changes how analysts interpret results: instead of inspecting forced topic lists, they review an explicit noise pool and decide whether peripheral literature merits separate attention. The deterministic audit trace lets researchers inspect noise and topic assignments directly.

\paragraph{Legacy Trade-offs}
Migration is not uniformly beneficial. Interactive tools offer immediate visual exploration, manual relabeling of clusters, and decades of community documentation that a command-line pipeline cannot match. A declarative workflow also demands comfort with a terminal and, for GPU acceleration, a compatible NVIDIA device. We therefore position OpenSciencePipe as a complement for reproducible, large-scale, or repeated analyses, while small one-off explorations may remain better served by a GUI.

\subsection{Open Science}
The concordance observed between OpenSciencePipe and published benchmarks originally executed on Web of Science or Scopus confirms the growing maturity of open scholarly metadata. By building on public endpoints such as OpenAlex and Crossref, the framework removes the dependency on expensive proprietary database subscriptions.
As demonstrated on corpora exceeding 50,000 publications, OpenSciencePipe executes without relying on commercial language model APIs. Combined with GPU network processing optimized for consumer graphics cards with 6~GB of memory, the entire workflow operates locally with no license or commercial API costs. This minimal barrier to entry enables independent scholars and institutions with limited research budgets to perform large-scale, fully reproducible science mapping.

Furthermore, this open accessibility provides direct practical utility for exploratory domain reconnaissance. Early-career researchers---including Master's and doctoral candidates entering unfamiliar scientific disciplines---often face dual bottlenecks: the institutional paywall barrier of proprietary commercial databases and the cognitive friction of manually triaging thousands of uncurated publications. By generating synchronized neural thematic clusters, citation lineages, and collaborative networks directly from open metadata at no license or commercial API cost, the framework provides an inspectable macro-level overview of a discipline. Early-stage investigators can thereby rapidly identify core conceptual foundations, trace key genealogical lineages, and distinguish active research fronts from low-density boundary literature before committing to exhaustive qualitative reading.

\subsection{Validity Bounds}
\label{sec:validity}
A primary methodological consideration in informetric replication across open scholarly infrastructure is the operational boundary between algorithmic validity and database coverage divergence \cite{visser2021large, martin2021google}. Because open bibliographic platforms (OpenAlex, Crossref) provide broader coverage of non-Anglophone scholarship, preprints, and open-access proceedings than selective commercial indices (Web of Science, Scopus), exact rank-order parity is neither expected nor theoretically desirable. As demonstrated across our benchmark evaluations, high thematic cluster recovery ($89.7\%$) and robust macro-structural alignment confirm that open metadata reliably reproduces the structural findings of proprietary scientometric studies without commercial vendor lock-in.

A second operational boundary involves author homonymy and entity splitting. While OpenSciencePipe disambiguates authors via the multi-signal protocol detailed in Section~\ref{sec:tier3}, residual name variants and homonymous merges remain visible in dense collaborative network topologies when common surnames lack persistent ORCID linkages or institutional metadata. Benchmarking automated disambiguation accuracy against verified gold-standard author registries (such as ORCID-curated sub-graphs) remains an important focus for ongoing development.

\subsection{Limitations}
While OpenSciencePipe automates the pipeline from API query to \LaTeX{} manuscript synthesis, human oversight remains essential for qualitative contextualization and domain-specific interpretation. Several engineering and methodological avenues will be pursued in future work. 

First, regarding dual interface ecosystems and GUI enhancements, ongoing work focuses on embedding interactive graph curation controls directly into the web visualizer studio, bridging headless cluster execution with exploratory visual filtering. Second, for multi-modal full-text PDF extraction, current bibliometric parsing extracts metadata from rich abstracts and open-access metadata harvested via Unpaywall and PMC; incorporating vision-language parsing will allow the autonomous extraction of high-resolution figures, tabular data, and citation context directly from complex multi-column PDFs. Third, the development of living bibliometric citation monitors utilizing persistent webhook triggers and scheduled API polling will enable dynamic monitoring that autonomously alerts researchers to newly indexed landmark publications, updating living science maps and collaborative networks in real time.

\paragraph{Operational constraints}
Three practical constraints bound the validated regime. (i)~\emph{Corpus size.} Density-based clustering with a fixed minimum cluster size ($m_{\text{cl}} = 10$) requires sufficient local document density in continuous embedding space. While the framework successfully extracts substantive macro-themes on compact collections (as shown across our 10 thematic validation benchmarks with $N = 100\text{--}400$), applying fixed $m_{\text{cl}} = 10$ to dispersed, low-density compact corpora risks over-delegating documents to Topic~$-1$ without forming clusters, demonstrating that collections below approximately 500 records benefit from adaptive $m_{\text{cl}}$ scaling or hierarchical fallback heuristics, and sparse results should not be read as evidence of thematic absence. (ii)~\emph{Hardware.} GPU acceleration presumes an NVIDIA device with CUDA support; the reported 6~GB VRAM envelope accommodates the 118{,}338-node co-authorship graph examined here but was not stress-tested beyond it, and CPU-only installations revert to a NetworkX path whose latency scales super-linearly above $10^5$ edges. (iii)~\emph{API quotas.} Federated harvesting is bounded by per-endpoint rate limits and polite-pool policies; large corpora require scheduled retrieval, and the corpus composition is therefore partially contingent on transient endpoint availability rather than on the query specification alone.

\subsection{Implications for Informetric Practice}
Beyond engineering efficiency, the consolidation demonstrated here carries a methodological consequence for open science. Constraining harvesting, graph analytics, and neural topic modeling to a single versioned provenance trail makes it possible to state the relationship between intermediate states explicitly. Under a declarative specification, the analyst's effort moves from configuring successive graphical parameters across fragmented user interfaces to formulating a well-posed query and auditing the resulting decisions---reviewing the explicit noise pool, the topic diversity, and the concordance metrics---which are all recorded artefacts rather than transient interface states. This makes the provenance of a science map an inspectable, regenerable object.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this paper, we introduced \textbf{OpenSciencePipe}, an integrated, open-source computational framework for automated bibliometric discovery and science mapping. OpenSciencePipe integrates multi-source API harvesting (OpenAlex, Crossref, Semantic Scholar, PubMed), programmatic Levenshtein deduplication, GPU-accelerated graph analysis via RAPIDS \texttt{cugraph}, contextual neural topic modeling via BERTopic with density-based noise rejection (Topic~$-1$), and automated publication-grade \LaTeX{} compilation.

Across an empirical validation suite of published benchmark corpora, the framework demonstrated high thematic cluster recovery against author-defined ground truth ($89.7\%$ vs.\ $51.7\%$ for classical Dirichlet baselines, Cohen's $\kappa = 0.895$) with explicit noise isolation, sub-second GPU community partitioning on co-authorship networks of over 118,000 nodes ($\approx 2{,}000\times$ speedup over CPU baselines with consistent Louvain modularity $Q \approx 0.90$), and macro-structural concordance with published geopolitical rankings. Dual head-to-head baseline comparisons confirm that density-based outlier isolation preserves distinct cluster vocabularies while isolating low-density boundary literature into an explicit noise pool.

Two boundary conditions delimit these claims. While macro-thematic discovery is demonstrated down to compact collections ($N \approx 100\text{--}400$), density-based clustering operates with greatest stability above roughly 500 documents; below that threshold, fixed parameterization ($m_{\text{cl}} = 10$) requires careful calibration to prevent dispersed documents from over-allocating into Topic~$-1$. By providing an open, high-performance, and reproducible foundation, OpenSciencePipe removes commercial database lock-in and manual workflow bottlenecks in computational informetrics, establishing a transparent foundation for reproducible science mapping.
